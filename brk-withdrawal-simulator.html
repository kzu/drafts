How it works

The rules

  • You buy the asset at the close of the month before your first withdrawal. The cash buffer (years × 12 × monthly spending) is set aside from the initial amount; the rest buys shares (or index units). Fractional shares are allowed.
  • Every month from the first withdrawal to the latest close, in order: cash earns one month of interest; the month is “down” if the close is below the all-time high by at least the trigger (the high includes every close in the asset’s data and is updated after the check).
  • Down month with enough cash: spending comes from cash. Otherwise spending comes from selling shares at that month’s close. If a down month finds the buffer short, the remaining cash is used and the rest is sold — that counts as a forced sale.
  • In a month that is not down, after spending, shares are sold to refill the cash buffer back to its target.
  • If shares plus cash can no longer cover a month, the portfolio is depleted: what is left is paid out and the value stays at zero.
  • Smoothed basis: for each year Y the annual rate is (close Dec Y−1 ÷ close Dec Y−6 − 1) ÷ 5. A synthetic price starts at the purchase close and grows by (1 + rate)1/12 each month; the same rules run on it, with the all-time high measured on the synthetic price. The first rate needs the asset’s first December close, so a smoothed run starts in January 1986 for BRK.A, 1994 for the S&P 500 and 2005 for QQQ at the earliest.

Assumptions and limits

  • No taxes, commissions or inflation adjustment; spending is a flat nominal amount. The S&P 500 is the index itself, with no fund fees; QQQ prices are net of the fund’s fees.
  • All trades happen at the month-end close; the last month is partial and uses the latest close (), with a full month of cash interest.
  • Dividends are reinvested: Berkshire pays none, so its price is its total return; the S&P 500 uses the total-return index (^SP500TR); QQQ uses Yahoo’s dividend-adjusted close, so its prices sit below the quoted ones in earlier years.
