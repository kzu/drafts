How it works

The page invests a lump sum, and optionally a monthly amount for up to 25 years, in Berkshire Hathaway or the S&P 500 at any month-end in their history, then takes a fixed amount out every month for 10 to 30 years, and shows how a cash buffer, spent only when the market is down, changes the outcome. The playback repeats the plan for every start date.

The rules

  • The initial amount buys the asset at the close of the month before the start date. Fractional shares (or index units) are allowed.
  • Investing period (optional): from the start date, the monthly investment buys more at every month-end close. The cash buffer (years × 12 × monthly spending) is set aside when the withdrawals are about to start: by selling shares at the close of the last investing month, or, without an investing period, out of the initial amount before it buys shares.
  • Today’s dollars (the default): the initial amount, the monthly investment and the monthly spending are dollars of the latest CPI month (). Each is multiplied by CPI(month) ÷ CPI(latest) to get the dollars of that month: the initial amount with the CPI of the purchase month, and the monthly investment, the spending and the buffer target with the CPI of every month of the run, so they keep the same buying power. Trades happen in the dollars of their month, at the actual closes; every amount shown is converted back to today’s dollars the same way, so values and withdrawals in different years compare directly. The playback’s return is then the real return, the asset’s growth divided by the growth of the CPI. Nominal uses the amounts as they are, every year.
  • A run lasts the investing period (0–25 years) plus the spending period (10–30 years) from the start date; when the data ends sooner it is cut at the latest close, and the page says so. Every chart, number and table on the page covers that window, and the charts tint the investing months and mark the first withdrawal with a line.
  • The playback steps the start date forward one year at a time with the asset you picked, keeping the month you picked, through the last start that still has a full window of data. With “Move the whole page” on, the whole page follows each step.
  • Every month of the withdrawals, in order: cash earns one month of interest; the month is “down” if the close is below the all-time high by at least the trigger (the high includes every close in the asset’s data, the investing months included, and is updated after the check).
  • Down month with enough cash: spending comes from cash. Otherwise spending comes from selling shares at that month’s close. If a down month finds the buffer short, the remaining cash is used and the rest is sold — that counts as a forced sale.
  • In a month that is not down, after spending, shares are sold to refill the cash buffer back to its target.
  • If shares plus cash can no longer cover a month, the portfolio is depleted: what is left is paid out and the value stays at zero.
  • Smoothed basis: for each year Y the annual rate is (close Dec Y−1 ÷ close Dec Y−6 − 1) ÷ 5. A synthetic price starts at the purchase close and grows by (1 + rate)1/12 each month; the same rules run on it, with the all-time high measured on the synthetic price. The first rate needs the asset’s first December close, so a smoothed run starts in January 1986 for Berkshire Hathaway and January 1994 for the S&P 500 at the earliest.

Assumptions and limits

  • No taxes or commissions. The S&P 500 is the index itself, with no fund fees.
  • Inflation is the US CPI for all urban consumers (CPI-U, all items, not seasonally adjusted), the monthly average of each month applied to its month-end. October 2025 was never published, after the federal shutdown, and uses the geometric mean of September and November 2025; months after use its CPI. Down months are still measured on the nominal price, the drop investors see.
  • All trades happen at the month-end close; the last month is partial and uses the latest close (), with a full month of cash interest.
  • Dividends are reinvested: Berkshire pays none, so its price is its total return; the S&P 500 uses the total-return index (^SP500TR).
  • Cash interest uses approximate annual averages of the 3-month US Treasury bill yield, applied monthly (rate ÷ 12). 1980–2005 and 2026 (to date) are averages of the daily 13-week T-bill index (^IRX):
